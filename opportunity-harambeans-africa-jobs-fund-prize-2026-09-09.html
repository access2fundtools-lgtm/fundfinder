Read This Before You Apply

  • The $1 million is not the prize. The prize is $20,000. This is the single most misread thing about this programme. The $20,000 is a grant. The $100,000 pilot money and the $100,000 to $1 million range after it are investment, they are not automatic, and you only reach them by proving the business works. Write your application for the $20,000.
  • "Build in Africa, sell to the world" is the whole test. A business serving only Nigerian customers is a weak fit no matter how good it is. They are looking for production in Africa reaching buyers beyond Africa.
  • Job creation is the point, not a bonus line. The fund is called the Africa Jobs Fund. Tie employment to real numbers — how many production staff, quality control, packaging, logistics and technical roles appear as orders grow. A vague promise to "create jobs" reads as filler.
  • Being Nigerian gets you eligible, not competitive. This is a continental competition, not a Nigeria-only scheme. Your Nigerian base helps only when you use it — access to a specific crop, a supply chain, a skills pool or a cost advantage.
  • Generic applications will not survive. "I want to start a clothing business" says nothing. Name the product, the production advantage, the overseas buyer, the expected demand and the job maths. Specificity is what separates an idea from an investment case.
  • Do not aim for 14 October. Use the 30 September early deadline as your real target. It gives you two weeks to fix what you got wrong rather than submitting whatever you had at midnight.

About This Opportunity

The Harambeans Africa Jobs Fund Prize is built on one commercial idea: African businesses should produce valuable goods and services in Africa and sell them to customers beyond the continent, creating real jobs on the way. That single filter separates it from the thousands of general small business grants where almost any idea can be submitted.

It is run by the Africa Jobs Fund with Harambeans, a pan-African entrepreneurial network. Winning brings you into the Harambeans Alliance — more than 400 entrepreneurs across 19 classes, whose ventures have collectively raised over $2 billion. For most winners the network is worth more over time than the cheque.

The funding is best understood as a sequence, not a single pot. You win $20,000 as a grant and join the Alliance. A business that then builds something credible, tests its market and shows commercial traction can be considered for up to $100,000 to pilot. One that clears that stage can be considered for $100,000 to $1 million to scale. Every stage after the first is investment and depends entirely on performance.

Unusually for a programme of this size, an early-stage venture qualifies. The stated criteria cover a compelling idea without an incorporated company, an overlooked business opportunity, an existing business needing capital, and founders in the diaspora planning to return to Africa and build. No polished business plan is demanded — the strength of the commercial logic carries the application.

Nigeria is a strong base for exactly this brief: large agricultural value chains, an established manufacturing community, deep consumer demand and a big skills pool. The winning move is usually to find a commodity or component that leaves the country raw or arrives imported, and to show credibly how it could be processed, manufactured or moved better from here.
